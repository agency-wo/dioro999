What 999.9 means: gold purity, karats and hallmarks

Two scales describe the same thing: how much of a piece is actually gold. Here is how to read both, what the stamps on a piece mean, and why almost no jewellery is pure gold.

Two scales, one meaning

Gold purity is written either in karats (parts of gold out of 24) or in fineness (parts of gold out of 1,000). The karat scale is the older jewellers' way; fineness is what refiners, mints and assay offices use. The table lines them up.

KaratFinenessGold contentWhere you meet it
24K999.999.99%Investment bars and coins
24K99999.9%Some coins, some Asian jewellery
22K91691.7%Sovereigns, Krugerrands, South Asian and Middle Eastern jewellery
18K75075.0%Fine jewellery in Europe and the US
14K58558.5%Everyday jewellery in the US
9K37537.5%Budget jewellery in the UK and Australia

The decimal in 999.9 is not decoration. A refiner guarantees at least 99.99 percent gold in a "four nines" bar; 999 (three nines) guarantees 99.9 percent. The difference is tiny in metal value but it is the difference between two standards, and serious bars are stamped with the one they meet.

Why jewellery is rarely 24K

Pure gold is soft. A 24K ring bends under a firm grip and its surface scratches in weeks of daily wear. Mixing gold with copper, silver, zinc, nickel or palladium makes an alloy that holds a shape, keeps a polish and can hold a stone safely. That is why rings and chains are usually 14K or 18K: roughly 60 to 75 percent gold, the rest doing the structural work. 22K is still common where tradition values the colour and the metal content over hardness.

Bars and coins have the opposite brief. Nobody wears them; they sit in a capsule or a safe, so they can be as pure as refining allows, and their value is simple to compute from weight and fineness.

In short

  • Karats count parts of 24; fineness counts parts of 1,000. 18K and 750 are the same thing.
  • 999.9 is the refining standard for bars and coins, not for jewellery you wear.
  • A stamp is a claim. Weight plus purity in writing, from a seller you can reach, is what protects you.

How to read a hallmark

Look inside the band of a ring, on the clasp of a chain, or on the back of a pendant. You will usually find one or more of these:

  • A fineness or karat mark: 750, 585, 375, or 18K, 14K, 9K. On bars: 999.9 or 999, often with "FINE GOLD".
  • A maker's or sponsor's mark: initials or a small symbol identifying who made or sold the piece.
  • An assay office mark: in countries with compulsory hallmarking (the UK, Ireland, the Netherlands, Switzerland for watch cases) an independent office tests the metal and adds its own symbol, sometimes with a date letter. The United States has no assay offices; a piece stamped "14K" must legally be what it says, and the seller is responsible for it.
  • A serial number on bars and on some coins, matched to an assay card.

Very small or very old pieces may carry only a fineness number, and a worn band may have lost its marks altogether. That does not make the gold fake; it just means the claim has to be checked another way.

When the stamp is not enough

A stamp can be applied to anything. If a price looks too good, or a piece came without paperwork, a jeweller can check it in minutes: an acid test scratches a tiny streak and reacts it with a known solution, and an XRF reader reads the alloy without touching the surface. Magnets are a weak test: gold is not magnetic, but neither are many base metals used in plated pieces.

At DiOro999.9 every listing states the purity and the weight, the gold is hallmarked, and bars and coins come sealed with their serial number. If you want a piece re-checked before you buy, ask; it is a fair question.

Questions people ask

Is 999.9 the same as 24K?

Almost. 24K means pure gold on the karat scale; 999.9 means 99.99 percent gold on the fineness scale, which is the refining standard for bars and coins. In practice a 999.9 bar is what people call 24K gold. Jewellery stamped 24K is usually 999 fine.

Can a hallmark be faked?

Yes. A stamp is only a claim, and a cheap base-metal piece can be stamped 750 or 18K. Buy from a seller who states the weight and purity in writing, and if in doubt have a jeweller test the piece with an acid kit or an XRF reader; both are quick and non-destructive.

Does purity change the colour?

Yes. Pure gold is a deep warm yellow. As the alloy metals rise, the colour changes: copper pushes it towards rose, silver and palladium towards white, and 9K or 14K yellow gold is paler than 22K. White gold is usually rhodium-plated on top, which wears over the years.
