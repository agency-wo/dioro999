Quartz or automatic: choosing a first watch

The first choice is not the brand or the dial. It is what is inside, because that decides how the watch behaves every day and what it asks of you.

How a quartz watch works

A battery sends a small current through a tiny quartz crystal, which vibrates at a very steady 32,768 times a second. A circuit counts those vibrations and steps the hands (or updates a digital display) once a second. There is almost nothing to wear out. A quartz watch typically keeps time to within 15 seconds a month, the battery lasts two to four years, and the watch costs less to make. Solar-charged quartz watches replace the battery with a rechargeable cell under the dial and run for decades on light.

How an automatic works

An automatic is a mechanical watch: a coiled mainspring stores energy, a train of gears lets it out, and a balance wheel swinging back and forth five to eight times a second meters the release. "Automatic" means a weighted rotor inside winds the mainspring as your wrist moves, so you do not wind it by hand (though you can). A good automatic keeps time to within 10 to 20 seconds a day, runs for about 40 hours off the wrist, and wants a service every five to seven years. What you get for that is a living mechanism: a sweeping seconds hand, no battery ever, and a watch that can be kept running for generations.

In short

  • Quartz: set it and forget it, very accurate, a battery every few years, lower cost.
  • Automatic: a mechanism you wind by wearing, less accurate, a service every 5 to 7 years, more to love.
  • Pick the case size for your wrist and the water rating for your life, then the dial you like.

Which one for a first watch?

If you want a watch that is always right and asks nothing, quartz. If you want a watch as an object, with a mechanism you can hear and feel and keep for life, automatic. Many people end up with one of each: a quartz for the week and an automatic for the weekend. Neither is the "better" answer; the wrong answer is an automatic bought by someone who will be annoyed the first time it is two minutes slow.

Size and strap

Case diameter is the number everyone quotes, but lug-to-lug (the length from strap end to strap end) decides whether a watch fits your wrist. A 36 to 40 mm case suits most wrists; 41 to 44 mm reads as a sports watch and needs a wider wrist to sit flat. Look at the thickness too: under 12 mm slips under a cuff. Straps come in widths from 18 to 22 mm; a metal bracelet is durable and heavier, leather is lighter and dressier, a NATO or rubber strap is for water and summer. Most straps can be swapped at home with a spring-bar tool.

Water resistance, in plain terms

The rating on the dial is a laboratory pressure test, not a promise about diving. Read it like this:

MarkingFine forNot for
3 bar / 30 mRain, hand washing, splashesSwimming, showers
5 bar / 50 mShowering, brief swimmingDiving, water sports
10 bar / 100 mSwimming, snorkellingScuba diving
20 bar / 200 mDivingSaturation diving

Whatever the rating, never pull the crown or press the buttons under water, rinse salt water off, and have the seals checked when the battery is changed or every few years; rubber gaskets age even if the watch is never wet.

Questions people ask

Does an automatic stop if I do not wear it?

Yes, once its power reserve runs out, usually after about 40 hours off the wrist (some run 70 hours or more). Nothing is harmed; you set the time and date and wind the crown a few turns or simply put it on. If you rotate between several watches, a winder keeps them running, but it is a convenience, not a necessity.

How often is a service due?

An automatic is a small machine with oils that age, so a service every 5 to 7 years keeps it accurate and protects the parts; sooner if it starts gaining or losing noticeably. A quartz watch needs a new battery every 2 to 4 years and little else; have the gaskets checked at the same time if it is going near water.

What does water resistance mean?

It is a pressure rating from a laboratory test, not a depth you can dive to. 3 bar or 30 m: splashes and rain only. 5 bar or 50 m: showering and brief swimming. 10 bar or 100 m: swimming and snorkelling. 20 bar or 200 m: diving. Never operate the crown or buttons under water, and have the seals checked after a battery change or every few years.
